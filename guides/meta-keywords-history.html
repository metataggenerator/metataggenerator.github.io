Meta Keywords: Why Nobody Uses Them Anymore

In the early days of search, the meta keywords tag was a simple idea: a page listed the words it should rank for, and the search engine believed it. That trust was abused within a few years, as site owners stuffed the tag with popular words that had nothing to do with their pages. The tag's fall from grace is a small piece of SEO history with a useful lesson in it.

This guide explains what the meta keywords tag was, why Google stopped using it, what other search engines do today, and what to use instead.

What the meta keywords tag was

The tag looked like this: <meta name="keywords" content="pizza recipe, homemade pizza, easy dinner">. It sat in the head of the page and listed the topics the page covered. In the mid-1990s, search engines used it as a signal for ranking, because pages had little other structured information to read.

It worked while site owners were honest. Then keyword stuffing began: pages listed hundreds of words, including competitors' brand names and trending terms, to rank for searches they did not deserve. The signal became noise, and the search engines stopped trusting it.

Why Google ignores it

Google announced in 2009 that it does not use the meta keywords tag in ranking at all. The company had already ignored it for years, because the tag told Google what the author claimed the page was about, while the visible content told Google what the page actually was. The visible content was always the more reliable signal.

Google's announcement was unusually blunt for the company, and it settled the question for most of the industry. Adding a keywords tag today does nothing for your Google rankings, positive or negative. It is simply ignored.

What other search engines do

Bing has said it may use the keywords tag as a minor signal, mainly to detect spam: a page whose keywords tag is stuffed with unrelated words can be treated with suspicion. Yahoo and other smaller engines have made similar statements over the years. None of them treats the tag as an important ranking factor.

The practical conclusion is the same everywhere: do not spend time on meta keywords. The effort is better spent on the title, the description, the visible content, and the Open Graph tags, all of which have real effects.

What to use instead

The modern replacement for meta keywords is not another tag: it is the visible content itself. Search engines read your headings, your first paragraph, and the words you use throughout the page to understand its topic. Write clearly about one topic per page, use the key terms naturally in the title and headings, and the search engine will understand.

If you need structured topic information, use schema markup, also called structured data. It describes the page in a format search engines trust, because it is checked against the visible content. That is the modern, honest version of what meta keywords tried to be.

Key takeaways

  • Google has ignored the meta keywords tag since at least 2009.
  • Keyword stuffing in the tag killed it: the signal became noise.
  • Bing may use it only as a spam signal, not a ranking boost.
  • Use clear visible content and schema markup instead of a keywords tag.

Frequently asked questions

Should I add a meta keywords tag to my pages?

No. Google ignores it completely, and no major search engine treats it as an important ranking factor. Spend the time on your title and content instead.

Can a meta keywords tag hurt my rankings?

Google ignores it, so it cannot hurt you there. Bing has said a stuffed keywords tag can look like spam, so if you use one, keep it short and honest.

What replaced the meta keywords tag?

Visible content and schema markup. Search engines understand a page from its title, headings, and text, and structured data adds trusted detail.
